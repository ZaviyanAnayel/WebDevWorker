1. How DNS Turns Names into IP Addresses

When you type example.com, your computer does not know where that is — it asks the Domain Name System, the internet's phone book. The lookup is a chain: your device asks a recursive resolver (usually your ISP or 1.1.1.1/8.8.8.8), which walks down from the root servers to the TLD servers (.com) to your domain's authoritative nameservers, which finally answer with the IP.

The whole chain usually completes in milliseconds, and resolvers cache answers to avoid repeating it. That caching is the source of both DNS's speed and its most confusing behavior — stale records — which we will unravel in section 3.

# Watch a full DNS lookup in action
dig example.com +trace

# Simple query: what IP does this name resolve to?
dig example.com +short
# 93.184.215.14

As a web developer you rarely run authoritative servers, but you constantly configure records at your registrar or DNS provider (Cloudflare, Route 53, Namecheap) — and when a deploy “doesn't work,” DNS is guilty often enough to earn this guide.

Think of the hierarchy as delegation: the root delegates .com to Verisign, Verisign delegates example.com to your nameservers, and your nameservers answer for everything beneath. Each level only knows about the one below it — elegant, distributed, and remarkably resilient.

2. The Record Types You Will Actually Use

DNS has dozens of record types, but six cover virtually all web development work:

TypeMapsExample Use
AName → IPv4 addressexample.com → 93.184.215.14
AAAAName → IPv6 addressIPv6 version of an A record
CNAMEName → another namewww → example.com
MXDomain → mail serversRoutes email to Google Workspace
TXTName → free textSPF, DKIM, domain verification
NSDomain → nameserversDelegates DNS to Cloudflare

The classic setup: an A record on the apex (example.com) pointing to your server's IP, and a CNAME on www pointing to the apex. One critical rule: a CNAME cannot coexist with other records, so it can never sit on the apex itself (which needs NS and SOA records) — use an A record or your provider's ALIAS/ANAME feature there.

MX and TXT records run your email: MX says where mail goes, while TXT records publish SPF, DKIM, and DMARC policies that keep your emails out of spam folders.

3. TTL and Propagation: Why Changes Take Time

Every DNS record carries a TTL (time to live) in seconds — how long resolvers may cache it. A TTL of 3600 means changes can take up to an hour to be visible everywhere. This delay is propagation, and it is the number one source of “but I changed it!” confusion.

The pro move: lower your TTL to 300 seconds a day or two before a migration, make the change, verify, then raise it back. Short TTLs during the move mean mistakes are correctable in minutes; long TTLs afterward mean faster lookups and less load on your nameservers.

# Check a record's remaining TTL from a public resolver
dig example.com @1.1.1.1

# Compare what different resolvers see (propagation check)
for r in 1.1.1.1 8.8.8.8 9.9.9.9; do
  echo "== $r =="; dig +short example.com @$r
done

Note that some ISPs ignore low TTLs and cache longer regardless — during critical migrations, patience plus verification beats repeated edits.

4. Troubleshooting DNS Like a Pro

DNS problems have a signature: the site works for you but not for a client, or HTTPS is fine on www but broken on the apex. Diagnose from the authoritative source outward with dig, nslookup, and host:

# Is the authoritative server answering correctly?
dig example.com @ns1.yourprovider.com

# Full record dump: A, MX, TXT in one view
dig example.com ANY +noall +answer

# Quick checks
nslookup example.com
host -t MX example.com

Work through this checklist: (1) query the authoritative nameserver directly — if it is wrong, fix it there; (2) check TTL to see how long stale caches persist; (3) look for typos and trailing dots; (4) confirm you did not put a CNAME on the apex; (5) verify nameserver delegation at your registrar matches your DNS provider. Tools like whatsmydns.net visualize propagation across global resolvers.

5. DNS Best Practices Before You Deploy

Treat DNS as part of your deploy checklist, not an afterthought. Before pointing a domain at a new server: lower the TTL 24–48 hours ahead, publish SPF/DKIM/DMARC TXT records if the domain sends email, and keep the old server answering until propagation completes — a premature shutdown strands users on stale caches.

# TXT records every sending domain should publish
# SPF: which servers may send mail
"v=spf1 include:_spf.google.com ~all"
# DMARC: what to do with failures
"v=DMARC1; p=quarantine; rua=mailto:dmarc@example.com"

Use a DNS provider with an API (Cloudflare, Route 53) so record changes can be scripted and version-controlled alongside infrastructure. Monitor with periodic dig checks or uptime monitors that resolve independently — and document your zone somewhere your future self can find at 2 AM.

Finally, enable DNSSEC if your provider supports it — it cryptographically signs your zone so resolvers can detect spoofed answers. It is a one-time setup that closes one of DNS's oldest trust gaps.

6. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

What is the difference between an A record and a CNAME?

An A record maps a name directly to an IP address (example.com to 93.184.215.14). A CNAME maps a name to another name (www to example.com), so it follows wherever the target points. Use A for the apex domain and CNAME for subdomains that should track another hostname.

How long does DNS propagation take?

Anywhere from minutes to 48 hours, depending on the TTL you set and how aggressively resolvers cache. With a 300-second TTL, most of the internet updates within minutes; some ISPs cache longer regardless. Plan migrations around the old TTL, not wishful thinking.

Can I use a CNAME on my root domain?

No. DNS rules forbid a CNAME from coexisting with the other records the apex requires (NS, SOA). Point the apex with an A/AAAA record, or use your provider's ALIAS or ANAME record, which behaves like a CNAME but is legal at the root.

What is a TTL in DNS?

Time to live: the number of seconds resolvers are allowed to cache a record before asking again. Low TTLs (300s) make changes propagate fast but increase query load; high TTLs (86400s) are efficient but slow to update. Lower it before migrations.

How do I check if my DNS changes have propagated?

Query multiple public resolvers with dig example.com @1.1.1.1 versus @8.8.8.8 and compare, or use a global propagation checker. Always verify against your authoritative nameserver first to confirm the source of truth is correct.
