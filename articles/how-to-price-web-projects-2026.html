1. Hourly vs Fixed vs Value-Based: Choosing Your Model

Hourly billing is the simplest to start: track time, multiply by rate, invoice. It protects you against scope uncertainty and is fair for exploratory or maintenance work. Its flaw is structural — it punishes efficiency. The faster and more experienced you become, the fewer hours you bill, which means your income stagnates exactly as your skill compounds.

Fixed pricing flips the incentive: you quote a price for an outcome, and every efficiency gain is profit. Clients love the budget certainty. But fixed pricing demands disciplined scoping — underestimate by 30 percent and you work the last month for free. It works best for well-defined builds where you have done something similar before and can predict the work accurately.

Value-based pricing anchors the price to the client's gain, not your cost. A booking system that saves a clinic $8,000 a month in no-shows is worth far more than the 40 hours it took to build. In practice, most successful freelancers blend models: fixed prices for defined projects, weekly or monthly retainers for ongoing work, and value framing in every proposal.

2. Scoping and Discovery: Where Accurate Pricing Starts

Every pricing disaster is a scoping disaster wearing a costume. Before quoting, run a discovery process: stakeholder interviews, content inventory, technical audit of existing systems, and a prioritized feature list split into must-have, nice-to-have, and later. For larger projects, sell discovery itself as a paid phase — a $500 to $2,000 blueprint that de-risks a $20,000 build for both sides.

Break the project into concrete deliverables with acceptance criteria, not vague phases. Design homepage (approved when client signs off on Figma), not Phase 1: Design. List explicit assumptions — client provides copy by Friday, third-party API docs exist, hosting credentials available day one — because every assumption is a future change order waiting to happen.

Then add a risk buffer: 15 to 25 percent for familiar work, more for anything novel. Beginners consistently underestimate by half because they price the happy path — coding — while forgetting revisions, client delays, deployment, documentation, and the three rounds of feedback. The buffer is not padding; it is the honest price of reality.

3. Calculating Your Rate: The Math

Work backwards from the life you need. Start with your target annual income, add business expenses (tools, insurance, accounting), add taxes (set aside 25 to 30 percent), add a profit margin and buffer for unpaid time off. Divide by your billable hours — not 2,000. Between marketing, admin, learning, and gaps between projects, most freelancers bill 800 to 1,100 hours a year, roughly half of full-time.

The formula: (target salary + expenses + taxes + buffer) divided by billable hours equals your floor hourly rate. If you want $90,000 take-home with $15,000 in expenses and taxes around 30 percent, you need roughly $150,000 in revenue; divided by 1,000 billable hours, your floor is $150 per hour. Quote project prices from this rate, then adjust upward for value and risk.

Sanity-check against the market, but never let the market set your floor — your costs are yours. Beginners often start 20 to 30 percent below their computed floor to win initial work, with a written plan to reach the floor within six months and exceed it within a year. Rates that never rise are a slow pay cut against inflation and growing skill.

// Freelance rate calculator — run anywhere
function calculateRate({ targetSalary, expenses, taxRate, billableHours, bufferPct }) {
  const grossNeeded = (targetSalary + expenses) / (1 - taxRate);
  const withBuffer = grossNeeded * (1 + bufferPct);
  const hourlyFloor = withBuffer / billableHours;
  return {
    revenueTarget: Math.round(withBuffer),
    hourlyFloor: Math.round(hourlyFloor),
    // Fixed-project sanity check: estimated hours * floor * value multiplier
    projectQuote: (estHours, valueMultiplier = 1.3) =>
      Math.round(estHours * hourlyFloor * valueMultiplier)
  };
}

const r = calculateRate({
  targetSalary: 90000, expenses: 15000,
  taxRate: 0.30, billableHours: 1000, bufferPct: 0.15
});
console.log(r);
// { revenueTarget: 172500, hourlyFloor: 173, projectQuote: [Function] }
console.log("40h project quote:", r.projectQuote(40)); // ~$9,000

4. Proposals, Deposits, and Payment Terms

A proposal is a sales document, not an estimate spreadsheet. Open with the client's problem in their words, present your recommended approach, show the investment with options (good, better, best tiers anchor the decision), and close with clear next steps. Three tiers work because most clients choose the middle — which is exactly where your ideal scope and price should sit.

Protect cash flow with structure: 30 to 50 percent non-refundable deposit before work starts, milestone payments tied to deliverables, and final payment due before or at launch — never 30 days after. State late fees (1.5 percent monthly is standard) and a work-pause clause for overdue invoices. Professional terms attract professional clients.

Add a kill fee: if the client cancels mid-project, they owe for work completed plus a percentage of the remainder. It sounds aggressive until the first time a project dies in week five and you are paid fairly anyway. Like deposits, it filters for serious clients before problems start.

5. Raising Prices Without Losing Clients

Raise your rates when any of these are true: your pipeline is full two months out, clients accept quotes without negotiation, or your skills and results have clearly outgrown your price. Most freelancers should raise rates annually at minimum; demand-driven raises of 15 to 25 percent are normal and healthy.

Grandfather existing retainer clients with notice, not surprise: thirty days' written notice framing it as an annual adjustment, with an option to lock the current rate by prepaying a block of work. You will lose a few price-sensitive clients — that is the point. They are replaced by better ones at the new rate, and your income rises while your hours stay sane.

The long-term escape from hourly thinking is productization: fixed-scope packages with fixed prices and streamlined delivery — website audits, landing page sprints, performance overhauls. Productized services sell while you sleep, deliver in days not months, and make raising prices as simple as updating a pricing page.

6. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

What should a beginner freelancer charge per hour?

Compute your floor rate from your costs first — most beginners land between 25 and 75 dollars per hour equivalent depending on location and niche. Start near the lower end to win initial work, then raise rates every few projects as your portfolio and confidence grow.

Fixed price or hourly for my first projects?

Hourly is safer while your scoping is unproven, since it protects you from underestimation. Move to fixed pricing as soon as you can scope reliably — it rewards your growing speed and clients prefer the budget certainty.

How do I handle scope creep on fixed-price projects?

Acknowledge the request warmly, point to the signed scope, and quote the addition as a separate change order with its own price and timeline. Put revision limits and a change-order process in every contract so this conversation is expected, not awkward.

Should I charge for discovery calls?

Initial 20 to 30 minute fit calls are free — they are sales. But deep discovery involving audits, stakeholder interviews, and a written blueprint should be a paid phase. Charging for discovery filters serious buyers and produces the scope your quote depends on.

What deposit percentage is standard for web projects?

Thirty to fifty percent upfront is standard, with the remainder tied to milestones. Larger projects often use 40/40/20 splits. Never start work without a deposit — it is the simplest filter for serious clients.

Keep leveling up

Pricing is a skill, not a guess. Scope honestly, charge for value, and raise your rates every year — your future self will thank you.
