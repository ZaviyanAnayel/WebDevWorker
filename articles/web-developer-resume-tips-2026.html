1. Format First: Beating the ATS and the 6-Second Scan

Before a human sees your resume, software usually does. Applicant Tracking Systems parse your document into structured fields, and exotic layouts — multi-column designs, text boxes, graphics, tables-as-layout — parse into garbage. A clean single-column document with standard headings (Experience, Projects, Skills, Education) sails through every parser and every six-second human scan.

Keep it to one page until you have roughly a decade of experience; two pages max for senior engineers. Use a standard font at 10.5 to 12pt, consistent date formatting, and generous margins. Export as PDF to freeze the layout — but generate that PDF from real text, not a scanned image, so the text remains selectable and parseable.

Your header is prime real estate: name, city, phone, email, and links to portfolio, GitHub, and LinkedIn as full clickable URLs. Skip the street address and skip the objective statement — a two-line professional summary targeting the role replaces it and actually gets read.

2. Bullet Points That Prove Impact

Every bullet should follow a simple formula: strong action verb, what you did, and the measurable result. Led migration of the checkout flow to React, cutting load time 43 percent and lifting conversion 6 percent will always beat responsible for checkout page maintenance. Hiring managers hire outcomes, not duties.

No metrics? Quantify scale and scope instead: the size of the codebase, the number of users, the team size, the traffic handled. Built REST API serving 2M requests per day with 99.99 percent uptime tells a story even without a before-and-after. Avoid adjectives about yourself — let the numbers imply excellent.

Tailor ruthlessly to each posting. Mirror the job description's keywords naturally — if they ask for TypeScript, GraphQL, and CI/CD, those exact terms should appear in your bullets where truthful. This is not gaming the system; it is helping both the ATS and the skimming recruiter see the match in seconds.

BEFORE (duties, no proof):
- Responsible for front-end development tasks
- Worked on the dashboard with a team
- Helped improve website performance

AFTER (action + scope + metric):
- Rebuilt analytics dashboard in React + TypeScript, cutting
  load time from 4.2s to 0.9s (Lighthouse 98)
- Led team of 3 migrating 200k-line JS codebase to TypeScript
  with zero production incidents
- Optimized image pipeline (AVIF + responsive sizes), reducing
  LCP 61% and bounce rate 9% on mobile

3. The Skills Section: What to List and What to Cut

Group skills by category — Languages, Frameworks, Tools, Cloud — instead of dumping one alphabetical soup. List what you would be comfortable being interviewed on tomorrow; every skill listed is fair game for a deep-dive question, and getting exposed on a listed skill is worse than omitting it.

Mine the job posting for keywords and make sure genuine matches appear verbatim. If the role wants Postgres, Docker, and CI/CD and you have them, they belong in the skills section exactly as written. This single habit dramatically improves both ATS match scores and recruiter skim-match rates.

Cut ruthlessly: outdated tech you would not take a job in, soft-skill filler like hard worker or team player, and inflated proficiency bars or percentages that mean nothing. Ten honest, relevant skills beat thirty padded ones — and leave room for the projects section that actually wins interviews.

4. Projects and Experience for Every Level

Students, bootcamp grads, and career changers should treat projects as experience — because for hiring purposes, they are. Give each project the full treatment: one-line description, tech stack, two to three impact bullets, and links to the live demo and code. Two substantial projects presented this way outweigh a thin internship bullet.

Mid-level and senior developers should shift the emphasis from what was built to what changed: revenue influenced, costs cut, incidents reduced, teams mentored, systems designed. Led, architected, and drove replace built and helped as your scope grows. Show trajectory — each role should read as bigger than the last.

Open source contributions deserve their own line when meaningful: merged PRs to known projects, maintained libraries with real users, or substantial documentation work. They prove you can read others' code, follow contribution norms, and collaborate asynchronously — exactly the skills remote teams screen for.

5. Mistakes That Get Resumes Rejected

The instant-rejection list is short but deadly: typos and grammar errors (proofread aloud, then have a human read it), photos on US applications (they trigger bias concerns and get binned), and dense walls of text with no whitespace. Fancy templates with graphics columns routinely break ATS parsing — pretty is the enemy of parseable.

Drop the relics: objective statements (replaced by a targeted summary), references available upon request (assumed), and full street addresses (city is enough). Never lie about employment dates, titles, or skills — background checks verify the first two, and interviews expose the third within minutes.

Finish with a pre-send ritual: export fresh PDF, check every link clicks through, run it through a free ATS parser to confirm clean extraction, and tailor the top third to the specific posting. Then have one employed developer review it — a single round of peer feedback catches more issues than ten self-reviews.

Finally, remember the resume is a marketing document with exactly one conversion goal: the interview. Every line should earn its place by increasing that probability. If a bullet does not prove skill, show impact, or match the role, cut it — white space and readability convert better than a second page of filler.

Pre-send checklist (copy into your notes):

[ ] One page (or two for 10+ years experience)
[ ] PDF with selectable text, all links clickable
[ ] No typos — read aloud once, human review once
[ ] Keywords from the posting appear verbatim
[ ] Every bullet: action verb + scope + metric
[ ] Skills limited to what you can defend live
[ ] Live demo + code links for top 2 projects
[ ] Contact header: name, city, phone, email, links
[ ] File name: Firstname-Lastname-Resume.pdf

6. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

How long should a web developer resume be?

One page for most developers, up to two pages once you have around ten years of experience. Recruiters skim for under ten seconds, so density of proof matters far more than length — cut anything that does not help win this specific role.

Should I include a photo on my resume?

No for US applications — photos can trigger bias concerns and many companies discard them on sight. In some European and Asian markets a professional photo is still customary, so follow local norms if applying there.

Do I still need a cover letter in 2026?

Rarely required, but a short tailored one helps for roles you truly want. Three paragraphs max: why this company, why you fit with one proof point, and a clear close. Never send a generic template — it hurts more than sending nothing.

How do I list bootcamp or self-taught experience?

Put it under Education or a Training section with the program name and dates, then let strong projects do the talking. Employers care about demonstrated ability; two substantial projects with live demos outweigh the credential line itself.

Should I put my GPA on my resume?

Only if you are a recent graduate with a strong GPA (roughly 3.5+) and little experience. Once you have internships or projects, drop it — nobody hires a working developer for their GPA.

Keep leveling up

A great resume gets you the interview; your skills win the offer. Iterate on both, and track which version gets callbacks.
