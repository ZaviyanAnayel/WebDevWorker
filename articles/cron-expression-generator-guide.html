Understanding Cron Expressions: The Basics

Cron expressions consist of five space‑separated fields that define when a command should run:

  • Minute – 0‑59
  • Hour – 0‑23 (24‑hour clock)
  • Day of month – 1‑31
  • Month – 1‑12 (or JAN‑DEC)
  • Day of week – 0‑7 (both 0 and 7 represent Sunday, or SUN‑SAT)

Each field can contain a single value, a range (e.g., 1-5), a list (e.g., 0,15,30,45), a step (e.g., */5), or the wildcard * which means “every possible value”. Cron runs on the system’s local time zone unless the environment overrides it.

The Core Problem This Solves: Decoding Unreadable Crontab Lines

Developers often encounter crontab entries that are hard to read, leading to:

  • Mis‑interpreting */5 as “at minute 5” instead of “every 5 minutes”.
  • Off‑by‑one bugs when mixing day‑of‑month and day‑of‑week fields.
  • Timezone mismatches that cause jobs to fire at unexpected hours.
  • Maintenance nightmares when a schedule is documented only as a cryptic string.

Common Use Cases for a Cron Schedule Explained

  • Nightly backups – 0 2 * * * (2 AM every day)
  • Daily report emails – 30 8 * * MON-FRI (8:30 AM on weekdays)
  • Cache clearing – */15 * * * * (every 15 minutes)
  • CI pipeline triggers – 0 */4 * * * (every 4 hours)
  • Database maintenance windows – 0 3 1 * * (3 AM on the first of each month)

Troubleshooting and FAQ

Why isn’t my cron job running?

Check the system’s cron daemon status, verify file permissions, and ensure the command works when executed manually.

What’s the difference between */15 and 0/15?

Both mean “every 15 minutes”, but 0/15 is non‑standard and may be rejected by some cron implementations.

How do day‑of‑month and day‑of‑week fields interact?

If both are restricted (not *), the job runs when either field matches. Use ? (in Quartz) or leave one field as * to avoid conflicts.

What timezone does cron use?

By default cron uses the server’s local timezone. Override it by setting the CRON_TZ variable at the top of the crontab.

How can I schedule a job every 90 minutes?

Use two entries: 0 */3 * * * and 30 */3 * * *, or a script that sleeps for 90 minutes inside a single hourly job.

Cron vs. systemd timers – which should I use?

Cron is simple and ubiquitous, but systemd timers offer better logging, dependency handling, and timezone control. Choose based on the complexity of your environment.
